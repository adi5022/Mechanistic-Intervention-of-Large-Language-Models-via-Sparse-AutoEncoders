## 1. Layman's Explanation (What We Did & Found)

### What we tried to do:
Imagine a student taking a quiz. When asked *"Where is the Eiffel Tower?"*, the student's top-of-mind guess is *"London"*, but they also have *"Paris"* sitting quietly in their head as a close second guess. 

We tried to build an automatic system (the **Causal Selector**) that would find the exact thought-direction in the model's brain that was supporting the correct answer ("Paris"). Our plan was to locate this thought-direction and turn its volume knob down, hoping that re-balancing the model's brain would make the right answer ("Paris") pop to the top.

### What actually happened (The Findings):
When we tested this across 22 different quiz questions, **it failed on 21 of them (a 4.55% success rate).** 

Why? Because of a simple logical error in our strategy:
* We identified the brain features that were **helping** the correct answer ("Paris") and turned **them** down. 
* By turning down the volume of the correct answer's helper, we made the correct answer even weaker, causing it to drop further down.
* The only reason it worked on the Eiffel Tower example is that the helper feature we turned down was a broad "city-predicting" feature that was helping *"London"* even more than it was helping *"Paris"*. When we muted it, *"London"* crashed completely, allowing *"Paris"* to win by default. But on other facts, muting the correct answer's helper just killed the correct answer entirely.

**Conclusion:** To fix factual errors reliably, we cannot turn down the helper of the *correct* answer. We must instead find the helper of the **incorrect competitor** (like "London") and turn *that* down, or we must **amplify** the correct answer's helper rather than muting it.

---

## 2. Technical Methodology & Hypothesis
The experiment tested whether multi-feature iterative ablation at a fixed strength (`0.3`, max `5` rounds) can successfully correct factual completion errors where the model possesses suppressed knowledge.

We ran the iterative loop on the 22 suppressed facts from ROME's `known_1000` dataset:
1. Identify the top causal feature for the target token.
2. Apply a soft-ablation hook at strength `0.3`.
3. Repeat up to `5` rounds or until target is top-1.

## 4. Next Steps & Corrective Methodology
Based on these findings, we will pivot to:
1. **Targeting the Competitor instead of the correct answer**: Modify the search query to find features driving the *top incorrect prediction* (clean top-1) and ablate those.
2. **Steering (Amplifying)**: Instead of ablation, test adding (steering) the features that help the target token to see if that works more reliably.
