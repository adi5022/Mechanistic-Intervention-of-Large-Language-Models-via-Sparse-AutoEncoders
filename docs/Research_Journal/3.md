**Date:** July 20, 2026

## 1. Layman's Explanation (What We Did & Found)

### What we tried to do:
In our previous attempt, we realized that turning down the volume of the correct answer's "helper" features in the model's brain just made the correct answer weaker. 

In this session, we corrected that strategy. Instead of muting the correct answer's helpers, we targeted the **incorrect competitor** (the wrong guess that the model currently wanted to output). 
- At each round, we checked what wrong answer the model was currently predicting.
- We scanned the model's active brain features to find which ones were boosting that specific incorrect guess.
- We then muted the top feature responsible for that wrong guess.
- If the model still predicted a wrong answer in the next round, we checked what the *new* wrong prediction was, and muted *its* top driver, repeating this until the correct answer won.

### What actually happened (The Findings):
By targeting the competitor's helpers instead of the correct answer's helpers, our **success rate tripled from 4.55% to 13.64%** (3 out of 22 facts corrected). 
Furthermore, for all successful corrections, the model required only **1 round** of ablation, and the specificity score was **100.00%** (meaning the intervention did not break unrelated facts).

### Key Insight: Factual vs. Grammatical Competitors
We observed a clear split in why some cases succeeded while others failed:
1. **Factual Competitors (Success)**: When the model predicts a specific wrong fact (e.g., predicting "London" instead of "Paris" for the Eiffel Tower, or predicting another city for Francesco Castellacci/Giulio Romano), the incorrect guess is driven by specific factual features. Muting these features immediately allows the correct fact to emerge.
2. **Grammatical Competitors (Failure)**: For many prompts (such as *"Eavan Boland was born in"*), the base model's top prediction is a general grammar token like `" the"` or `" in"`. Because these tokens are general syntactical completions, they are supported by many general syntax features rather than a single fact-specific competitor feature. Muting them is like trying to empty an ocean with a bucket—the model's probability of predicting `" the"` barely budges, and we run out of rounds.

## 2. Technical Methodology
- **Intervention**: Identifying features driving the model's current top predicted token ID (using `get_top_competitor_features`) and applying persistent ablation hooks at strength `0.3`.
- **Dataset**: 22 suppressed facts from ROME's `known_1000`.
- **Evaluation**: Success rate, rounds used, and specificity check (15 control prompts).

## 4. Future Directions
Since competitor ablation successfully corrects facts when there is a factual competitor but struggles when the top prediction is a general syntax token:
1. **Targeting non-syntax tokens**: Filter or rank the competitor features excluding general grammatical features, or run competitor ablation only when the top competitor is not a stopword/punctuation/pronoun.
2. **Steering (Activation Patching / Amplification)**: Directly steering the correct target feature instead of trying to suppress the competitors.
