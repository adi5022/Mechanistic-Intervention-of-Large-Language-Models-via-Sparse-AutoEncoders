\chapter{Evolution of the System: a Record of Trial and Error}\label{ch:evolution}

This chapter tells how we arrived at the system described in Chapter~\ref{ch:method}. For each stage we say what we tried, what happened and why we moved on, following the order of the research journal (Appendix~A maps journal entries to sections). The early experiments used one prompt or 22 facts. They explain why the design looks the way it does, but they are not performance estimates.

\section{The Starting Point}\label{sec:start}

The first prototype (July 2026, journal 1) loaded GPT-2 Small and the layer-8 SAE and added a \emph{causal selector}. It took the features active on the last token of the prompt, removed each one in turn, and ranked them by how much the target's probability changed. Three things we learned there shaped the rest of the work. The most active feature is not the one that changes the answer, so we rank by causal effect and not by activation. Removing a feature completely damaged other predictions, whereas a partial change (strength 0.2 to 0.3) did not on the one example we tried. And an \emph{eligibility rule} applied to 45 fact pairs from ROME's \texttt{known\_1000} gave 15 already correct, 22 \emph{suppressed} (target not first but probable) and 8 improbable. The roadmap we wrote then already listed a learned predictor of which features to edit, which we reached in Section~\ref{sec:learned}, and a comparison with ROME, which is still open.

\section{Single-Feature Ablation}\label{sec:ablation}

Our first search ablated, one after another, the features that \emph{help} the target (strength 0.3, up to five rounds) on the 22 suppressed facts (journal 2). It corrected \textbf{1 of 22}. The reason is simple in hindsight: muting what helps the target weakens the target. The second version muted the drivers of the current \emph{competitor}, the token ranked first. It corrected \textbf{3 of 22}, each in one round and with no change to 15 control prompts (journal 3). All three started at rank 2, though, and the method failed whenever the leader was a generic word such as `` the'' (Figure~\ref{fig:early}a). One feature was not enough, and we wanted to know why.

\section{Several Competitors, Spread-Out Support}\label{sec:several}

For a probe prompt (``The location of Massachusetts Institute of Technology is in'', target `` Cambridge'', rank 8, with seven words above it) we muted all the competitors in proportion to their probability (journals 4 to 8). The seven competitors collapsed onto just two features: 313 for `` the'', `` a'' and `` an'', and 21169 for `` question'', `` jeopardy'', `` danger'' and `` doubt''. The target stayed at rank 8. Raising the strength on feature 313 by 73\% moved the target's probability only from 1.29\% to 1.39\%, so muting too weakly was not the problem. When we looked at each competitor's top ten features, the semantic competitors turned out to be spread over many of them, with the second feature 92 to 95\% as influential as the first. Muting the top three features per competitor reached rank 6, and five diluted the effect back to rank 7.

\section{Mute and Boost Together}\label{sec:hybrid}

Adding the opposite move, boosting the features that support the target, changed things at once. Three mutes and three boosts brought the probe prompt to \textbf{rank 1} (journal 5; Figure~\ref{fig:early}b). A fourth boost pushed it back to rank 2. This was not a bug, since an isolated rerun gave the same 6.7357\%. The fourth feature supports `` the'' more than `` Cambridge'', and because probabilities pass through a softmax, more editing is not always better. The lesson was to report the \emph{best} step and not the last. The hybrid edit with a safety filter (rank 2 on this prompt, safer but weaker) became our working algorithm.

\section{Where to Edit, and Making It Fast}\label{sec:depth}

A layer sweep on 21 prompts (12 of them eligible) showed a plateau at layers 6 to 9, with layer 8 highest. Its mean rank gain was 7.33, against 7.08, 6.50 and 6.33 at layers 7, 6 and 9, and at most 4.75 at the other layers (journals 10 and 11). We kept layer 8 as a reasonable default, not as a proven optimum.

The first real engineering step was a cost audit. A single prompt at one layer needed \textbf{186 model calls}. Both safety filters were recomputing the clean baseline inside every candidate loop, and passing it in removed 60 calls with identical results. Stacking the candidates into the batch dimension of one call then brought the number down to \textbf{6}, a 21-fold reduction. The ordered feature lists, the probabilities (to $10^{-9}$) and all 60 safety decisions matched the sequential code, which we kept as the reference. A CUDA GPU gave another factor of 3.8, and in the application the whole Hybrid run fell from about 12 s to under 1 s (Figure~\ref{fig:speed}).

\section{Making the Sweep Reliable}\label{sec:reliable}

Once the sweep was fast, its weaknesses became visible (journals 16 to 19). It reported the last step instead of the best one, which we fixed with best-so-far tracking. Applying mute and boost as two chained hooks gave rank 66 where the combined safety check gave 59, so we switched to a single combined hook. And it stopped early, because its pool held only features that had passed the filter against the \emph{clean} model. \textbf{Pool refill} fixed that by re-ranking against the edited model, and it exposed a hard limit. For ``The leading cause of death in men is'' only 67 features were active at the last token, and scaling cannot activate a feature that is off. The run used all 67 and stopped at rank 51 (from 152).

The fix was to draw candidates from \textbf{all prompt positions}, since the meaning of ``Sophia'' sits on the word \emph{Sophia} and not on the last word of the prompt. On 13 comparable prompts the all-positions version reached rank 1 on \textbf{10}, against 4 for the last token. It won on 9 prompts and lost on none (sign test $p\approx0.004$), and in all 9 prompts where the last-token version failed, it had used every feature it could see (Figure~\ref{fig:candsrc}; journal 20). We then asked whether the strict safety rule was too strict. In a 45-prompt pilot, removing the filter lost 10 of 29 successes and gained none, a 5\% tolerance lost 2, and graded strengths changed nothing. On 131 last-token prompts, relaxed rules added at most one prompt. Only 2.5 to 2.9\% of features were rejected on both sides, so the supply of features, and not the filter, was the real limit, and the strict filter stayed.

\section{Is the Layer, or the SAE, the Limit?}\label{sec:repair}

We tested three worries on the prompts that had failed in every mode (journal 22). Later layers might rebuild what the edit removes. The SAE might be more machinery than a factual edit needs. And the SAE's reconstruction error, which we never edit, might hold the missing information. The pushed direction was still 0.79 to 1.29 of its injected size at the output, and re-adding whatever fraction had been lost gave the same rank in 21 of 21 layer and prompt cells, so \textbf{we saw no repair}. A free residual edit of the same size reached rank 1 in all 21 cells, against 1 for the SAE edit. We chose these prompts because the SAE edit had failed on them, so this is a yardstick and not a success rate. A literature check the same week (journal 25) found mixed evidence on SAE steering and led us to compare every later result with controls.

\section{Scaling Up: the CounterFact Benchmark}\label{sec:scale}

Our hand-assembled sets (22, 45 and 131 prompts) share templates and are too small for held-out tests, so we built a benchmark from CounterFact (journal 24). Of its 21,919 records, 16,360 have a single-token true answer that GPT-2 Small ranks between 2 and 1000. We split them so that a subject appears in only one set and five relations are held out, which gave 1,000 training, 150 validation and 300 test prompts. For 1,450 of these prompts we cached the layer-8 states, the candidate features, their removal effects and the safety filter's verdict at ten strengths, once and for all. A \textbf{headroom} measurement then asked how much room any choice of multipliers has. Free per-feature multipliers could bring the target to rank 1 on \textbf{80\%} of the 1,450 prompts if side effects are ignored, and on 41\% with a side-effect penalty, against 6 to 15\% for crude fixed rules (Figure~\ref{fig:headroom}).

\section{Learning the Strengths}\label{sec:learned}

The sweep uses one mute strength (0.6) and one boost strength (0.5) for every prompt, even though the strengths change the outcome. We trained a small network to choose them (journals 23, 26 and 27). \emph{PromptNet} reads 12 summary numbers about the prompt, such as the target's rank and probability, and outputs a mute and a boost strength. \emph{FeatureNet} outputs one multiplier per candidate. Training back-propagated through blocks 8 to 11 from the cached states. We compared them with a \emph{learned fixed pair} (two numbers shared by all prompts) and a \emph{per-prompt oracle} tuned by gradient descent at test time. On the 300 test prompts, under a stand-in measure, fixed 0.6/0.5 reached rank 1 on 75 (25.0\%), the learned pair (mute 0.98, boost 1.81) on 116 (38.7\%), PromptNet on 119 and the oracle on 123 (Figure~\ref{fig:learned}). The gain came from a better \emph{global} pair. Choosing per prompt added only 3 prompts (exact McNemar $p=0.25$), side effects roughly doubled, and FeatureNet (31 to 32\%) stayed below the pair. We never ran the real sweep with the learned strengths. The network turned out not to be the useful part. Its \emph{teacher} was: free multipliers tuned on each prompt reached 73\% in the same stand-in, nearly twice what the network managed.

\section{Gradient Descent Enters}\label{sec:gd-enters}

Gradient descent was in the project for a week before it became the method. It first appeared as a measuring stick, as the bound for an edit made directly in the residual stream (journal 22, Adam on a free vector). It came back in the headroom measurement (journal 24) and in the oracle (journal 27). The oracle's 73\%, against 31 to 32\% for the network, suggested using the optimiser itself as the editor (journal 28, October 3). It gives one multiplier per candidate, tuned for the prompt by 100 Adam steps, in about 6 to 8 s per prompt. Section~\ref{sec:lit-gd} traces the idea in the literature.

The first hand trials favoured gradient descent but were unfair, because it could scale features from 0 to 3 times and the sweep only from 0.4 to 1.8. So the controlled comparison (journal 29) gave the sweep the same range (mute 1.0, boost 2.0) and measured all arms with one yardstick through the real hook. On the 300 test prompts gradient descent brought the true answer to rank 1 on \textbf{271} (90\%), the wide sweep on 161 and the original sweep on 94 (Figure~\ref{fig:gdband}). It solved 110 prompts the wide sweep missed and lost none. Its same-prompt KL was lower on 160 of the 161 prompts that both solved, and it needed 7.7 s per prompt against 38.5 and 49.7 s. Two cautions apply. Part of the KL advantage is built in, since its loss contains a KL term and the sweep has none. And the median target probability at rank 1 is only 7.7\%.

\section{Forcing Every Target to Rank 1: the Additive Edit}\label{sec:additive-story}

The 29 prompts that gradient descent failed on are mostly deep targets whose helpful features may be silent, and a multiplier cannot switch on a silent feature. The additive edit of Section~\ref{sec:additive-method} can, and its first hand trials looked wonderful. All six hard prompts we tried reached rank 1, including absurd targets such as a random name after ``Hello, my name is''. We ran a control that paired 20 validation prompts with random, unrelated words. Multipliers reached rank 1 on 1 of 20, but the additive edit reached it on \textbf{20 of 20}, using an edit 126 to 192\% the size of the residual stream itself (Figure~\ref{fig:addctl}). This is the analogue of fitting random labels \cite{r52} or of an adversarial perturbation \cite{r50,r51}: with enough freedom, an optimiser reaches any output you ask for. The edit also carried over to reworded prompts about as well for a random word as for the true answer (median rank 4 against 4.5, and rank 1 on 25\% against 16\%), and it changed the top word on 82\% of unrelated prompts. The edit \emph{steers toward a word} and does not repair a fact.

Three things followed. Rank 1 is a weak test, so every later result needs controls. Being able to steer a target to the top tells us nothing about knowledge in either direction, so we withdrew the early label ``knowledge absent'' as a statement about the model. And we switched the additive edit off by default and added a warning. It answered the question ``can we force GPT-2 to say what we want?'' with ``almost anything'', which is exactly why it is not a fix.

\section{What the Edit Does Beyond the Next Word}\label{sec:beyond}

We then tested the multiplier edit on 40 test prompts (10 per band) for what it does besides reaching rank 1 (journal 30). For \emph{reworded prompts}, we tuned the edit on one prompt and applied it unchanged. It lowered the true answer's median rank from 94 to 36 and reached rank 1 on 9\% of the rewordings, while a random word went from 17,504 to 11,832 and reached it on none. So there is a modest fact-related part, but the edit is mostly local. On \emph{unrelated prompts} the multiplier edit changed the top word of 6\% of 20 prompts, and the additive edit 17 to 24\%. For \emph{generated text} (20 greedy tokens, edit kept on), fluency was unchanged (perplexity 4.4 to 4.8 against 4.7), variety dropped slightly (distinct 2-grams 0.85 to 0.89 against 0.93), loops appeared in 2 to 12\% of continuations against none, and the target word was repeated about 0.6 to 0.7 extra times. For \emph{instructions}, we added the prefix ``Do not say the word X''. The unedited model already put X first on 45\% of prompts, because a named word primes copying. The multiplier edit raised this to 68\% and the additive edit to 88 to 90\%, so the edit ignores the instruction.

Table~\ref{tab:kept} sums up the record. A transient SAE edit brings a suppressed answer to the top on most held-out prompts, cheaply and with small side effects on the same prompt. It does this by pushing toward a word, it carries little beyond the prompt it was tuned on, and it ignores instructions. Three limits remained. An edit is tied to one model that has an SAE, GPT-2 Small lacks answers that larger models hold, and rank 1 needs controls to mean anything. These led us to a question for the tools we had built: could the change an edit makes in one model be carried into another? Chapter~\ref{ch:transfer} reports that study.
