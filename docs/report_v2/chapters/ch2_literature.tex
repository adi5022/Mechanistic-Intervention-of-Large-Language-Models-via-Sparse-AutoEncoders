\chapter{Literature Review}\label{ch:literature-review}

This chapter reviews the research threads on which the project draws, places the project's use of gradient descent in that literature, and states the gap. Most sources were examined through their abstracts and summaries, not in full; the numbers quoted are the sources' own.

\section{Knowledge Editing}

ROME \cite{r2} locates a factual association in the mid-layer MLPs of GPT-style models and rewrites one weight matrix in closed form; MEMIT \cite{r3} extends this to thousands of edits. Both change the weights permanently. Gupta \textit{et al.} \cite{r4} showed that sequential edits first erase earlier edits gradually and then collapse the model abruptly, and Hase \textit{et al.} \cite{r30} found that where a fact is localised need not predict where it can be edited best. ROME also contributed \textbf{CounterFact}, which pairs each prompt with a true and a counterfactual answer and supplies reworded (paraphrase) and neighbouring (neighbourhood) prompts for testing generalisation and specificity, and the \texttt{known\_1000} set of facts its model already predicts; ROME pre-filters facts in this way, and so do we. Methods that leave the weights alone add the fact as text: in-context editing \cite{r31} places the corrected fact and demonstrations in the prompt, and retrieval-augmented generation \cite{r34} conditions the model on retrieved documents. Both change the input, not the model's internal computation. EasyEdit \cite{r32} collects these methods in one framework and is the intended route for baselines we have not run.

\section{Activation Steering and Sparse Autoencoders}

Activation steering adds a direction to the residual stream at inference time: Activation Addition \cite{r9} uses the difference of two prompts' activations, contrastive activation addition \cite{r10} averages such differences, and representation engineering \cite{r11} reads and controls population-level directions. Because superposition \cite{r5} makes single neurons rarely correspond to single concepts, these directions carry unrelated content along. Sparse autoencoders \cite{r6,r7,r8} learn a wide sparse basis whose features are often interpretable; open SAEs exist for GPT-2 Small \cite{r15} and Gemma \cite{r19}, with browsable explanations \cite{r20}. Several studies edit SAE features directly: Chalnev \textit{et al.} \cite{r28} target features to limit side effects of steering, Zhao \textit{et al.} \cite{r29} edit a few features to control whether a model uses its memory or the context, Farrell \textit{et al.} \cite{r27} unlearn knowledge by feature ablation, Marks \textit{et al.} \cite{r33} use features as nodes of editable circuits, and Arad \textit{et al.} \cite{r23} show that choosing features by their effect on the output improves steering two- to threefold, which is the idea behind our causal ranking. The evidence is mixed. AxBench \cite{r12} reports that simple baselines can beat SAEs for steering, a Google DeepMind update \cite{r24} reports negative SAE results on downstream tasks, Chaudhary and Geiger \cite{r25} find SAEs struggle to reach a neuron baseline on factual knowledge in GPT-2 Small, and Cui \textit{et al.} \cite{r26} show that behaviour suppressed by clamping SAE features can be recovered through the part of the activation the SAE does not explain. None of these tests our exact setting, but together they are why we measure side effects and controls and assume nothing about a feature edit.

\section{Causal Localisation and Attribution}

Activation patching measures the causal effect of an internal component \cite{r21}. Because exhaustive patching is expensive, gradient-based approximations exist: attribution patching and AtP$^*$ \cite{r16} estimate component effects from one backward pass. We rank features by actual ablation, evaluated in batches (Section~\ref{sec:selection}), and keep gradient-based ranking as an option for larger fact sets. The logit lens \cite{r37} reads an intermediate state through the output projection and is used in our diagnostics.

\section{Gradient-Based Optimisation of Edits and Steering Vectors}\label{sec:lit-gd}

The project reached gradient descent from its own data (Section~\ref{sec:gd-enters}). It is, however, an old tool in this area, and the following lines of work place our use of it.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ROME itself.} Before it changes any weight, ROME finds the vector to be written by gradient descent on the probability of the new answer, with a term that keeps the subject's meaning unchanged \cite{r2}. Our loss has the same two ingredients (reach the target, limit the change elsewhere); the difference is that we tune a handful of SAE multipliers per prompt and change no weight.
  \item \textbf{Soft prompts and prefix tuning.} Lester \textit{et al.} \cite{r46} and Li and Liang \cite{r47} keep the model frozen and train continuous vectors by gradient descent, which shows that a frozen model can be steered through a small optimised input.
  \item \textbf{Optimised steering vectors.} Subramani \textit{et al.} \cite{r48} find vectors by gradient descent that, added to the hidden states of GPT-2, make it produce a chosen sentence nearly perfectly; Dunefsky and Cohan \cite{r49} optimise a steering vector from one training example and study how far it generalises, including to harmful behaviour.
  \item \textbf{Adversarial examples.} Szegedy \textit{et al.} \cite{r50} and Goodfellow \textit{et al.} \cite{r51} showed that a small perturbation found by gradient descent can force a network to any chosen output, and Zhang \textit{et al.} \cite{r52} showed that networks can fit random labels. Both are the right comparison for our additive edit (Section~\ref{sec:additive-story}): if an optimiser is given enough freedom, reaching the chosen output proves little.
  \item \textbf{The optimiser.} All our tuning uses Adam \cite{r36}.
\end{itemize}
As far as our limited search found, none of these tunes one multiplier per \emph{SAE feature} for a single prompt with a side-effect term and compares it with a hand-built feature sweep on held-out prompts.

\section{Comparing and Stitching Representations}

Model stitching connects the lower layers of one network to the upper layers of another through a small learned layer; if the stitched network still works, the two representations are compatible \cite{r41,r42}. Representational similarity measures such as CKA \cite{r43} compare activations without stitching. For language models, Chen \textit{et al.} \cite{r44} show that a simple affine map between the residual streams of two models transfers features at low cost: they move SAEs from a smaller to a larger model (cutting the larger SAE's training cost by about half) and recover probes and steering vectors. Our translator (Chapter~\ref{ch:transfer}) is a ridge regression \cite{r45} of this family and is not claimed as new.

\section{Research Gap}

Knowledge editing changes weights or the prompt; SAE steering works inside one model; stitching work transfers vectors learned in one model. To our knowledge, based on a limited search, the following were not found together: a joint mute-and-boost edit of causally ranked SAE features for a single fact with a per-feature safety filter and pool refill; a comparison on held-out CounterFact prompts, under one yardstick, between a hand-built sweep and per-feature gradient descent; a control showing that an additive edit forces unrelated words to rank 1; and a test of whether the change an edit makes in one model can be carried into another, with pass bars fixed beforehand. This report supplies these, including the negative results. It does not claim that the SAE route beats prompting, probes, difference-of-means steering, ROME or in-context editing; that comparison has not been made.
