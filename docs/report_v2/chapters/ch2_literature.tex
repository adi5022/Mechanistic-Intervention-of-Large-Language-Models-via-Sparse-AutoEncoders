\chapter{Literature Review}\label{ch:literature-review}

This chapter covers the work the project builds on, shows where our use of gradient descent sits in that literature, and states the gap we address. We read most sources through their abstracts and summaries rather than in full, and the numbers quoted are the sources' own.

\section{Knowledge Editing}

ROME \cite{r2} finds where a fact is stored in the middle MLP layers of GPT-style models and rewrites one weight matrix in closed form. MEMIT \cite{r3} extends this to thousands of edits. Both change the weights for good. Gupta \textit{et al.} \cite{r4} showed that editing one fact after another slowly erases the earlier edits and then suddenly collapses the model, and Hase \textit{et al.} \cite{r30} found that where a fact is stored does not necessarily tell you where it is best edited. ROME also gave us \textbf{CounterFact}, which pairs each prompt with a true and a counterfactual answer and adds reworded (paraphrase) and neighbouring (neighbourhood) prompts for testing generalisation and specificity \cite{r2,r65}. It also gave us the \texttt{known\_1000} set of facts that its model already predicts, and like ROME we filter our facts in this way. Chapter~\ref{ch:rome} describes the method and its scores in detail. Some methods leave the weights alone and add the fact as text instead. In-context editing \cite{r31} puts the corrected fact and a few demonstrations in the prompt, and retrieval-augmented generation \cite{r34} conditions the model on retrieved documents. Both change the input and not the model's own computation.

\section{Locating Facts and Measuring Edits}

ROME rests on two ideas from earlier work. The first is that the feed-forward (MLP) layers of a transformer act as key-value memories, in which a key pattern in the input selects a stored value \cite{r60}. The second is causal mediation analysis, which asks how much of an effect passes through a given internal component \cite{r59,r58}. ROME applies it as \emph{causal tracing}: corrupt the subject, restore clean states one place at a time, and see which restorations bring the answer back \cite{r2}. Activation patching, the general technique behind this, is surveyed by Heimersheim and Nanda \cite{r21}. The residual stream, to which every layer adds its output, is the basic object of this view of the transformer \cite{r53}.

Edits are measured on three questions \cite{r2}. \emph{Efficacy} asks whether the new answer beats the old one on the edit prompt, \emph{generalisation} whether it does so on reworded prompts, and \emph{specificity} whether unrelated facts about other subjects are left alone. These become the Efficacy, Paraphrase and Neighbourhood scores that Chapter~\ref{ch:rome} defines, and the same three questions are used by later editing benchmarks and toolkits \cite{r3,r32}. We use them for the same reason as the authors: a change that works on the edit prompt alone, or that spoils neighbours, is not an edit worth having. Distinct-$n$ counts \cite{r62} and a look at repetition loops \cite{r61} are the tools we use for the quality of generated text.

\section{Activation Steering and Sparse Autoencoders}

Activation steering adds a direction to the residual stream at inference time. Activation Addition \cite{r9} takes the difference between the activations of two prompts, contrastive activation addition \cite{r10} averages many such differences, and representation engineering \cite{r11} reads and controls directions that describe a whole population of examples. Superposition \cite{r5} means single neurons rarely match single concepts, so these directions tend to carry unrelated content with them. Sparse autoencoders \cite{r6,r7,r8} learn a wide basis in which only a few features are active at once, and those features are often interpretable. Open SAEs exist for GPT-2 Small \cite{r15} and Gemma \cite{r19}, with browsable feature descriptions \cite{r20}.

Several studies edit SAE features directly. Chalnev \textit{et al.} \cite{r28} aim steering vectors at particular features to limit side effects. Zhao \textit{et al.} \cite{r29} edit a few features to decide whether a model relies on its memory or on the context. Farrell \textit{et al.} \cite{r27} unlearn knowledge by ablating features, and Marks \textit{et al.} \cite{r33} use features as the nodes of editable circuits. Arad \textit{et al.} \cite{r23} show that picking features by their effect on the output improves steering two to three times, which is the idea behind our causal ranking. The evidence is mixed, though. AxBench \cite{r12} reports that simple baselines can beat SAEs at steering, and a Google DeepMind update \cite{r24} reports negative SAE results on downstream tasks. Chaudhary and Geiger \cite{r25} find that SAEs struggle to reach a neuron baseline on factual knowledge in GPT-2 Small, and Cui \textit{et al.} \cite{r26} show that behaviour suppressed by clamping SAE features can come back through the part of the activation the SAE does not explain. None of these tests exactly our setting. Together they are the reason we measure side effects and use controls, and why we assume nothing about what a feature edit does.

\section{Causal Localisation and Attribution}

Activation patching measures how much an internal component matters for the output \cite{r21}. Trying every component is expensive, so gradient-based shortcuts exist: attribution patching and AtP$^*$ \cite{r16} estimate component effects from a single backward pass. We rank features by actually removing them, in batches (Section~\ref{sec:selection}), and keep the gradient-based version in mind for larger fact sets. The logit lens \cite{r37} reads an intermediate state through the output projection, and we use it in our diagnostics.

\section{Gradient-Based Optimisation of Edits and Steering Vectors}\label{sec:lit-gd}

We came to gradient descent through our own data (Section~\ref{sec:gd-enters}). It is an old tool in this area, though, and the work below shows where our use of it fits.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{ROME itself.} Before ROME changes any weight, it finds the vector to be written by gradient descent on the probability of the new answer, with a term that keeps the subject's meaning intact \cite{r2}. Our loss has the same two ingredients, reaching the target and limiting the change elsewhere. The difference is that we tune a few hundred SAE multipliers for one prompt and change no weight.
  \item \textbf{Soft prompts and prefix tuning.} Lester \textit{et al.} \cite{r46} and Li and Liang \cite{r47} freeze the model and train continuous vectors by gradient descent, which shows that a frozen model can be steered through a small optimised input.
  \item \textbf{Optimised steering vectors.} Subramani \textit{et al.} \cite{r48} use gradient descent to find vectors that, added to GPT-2's hidden states, make it produce a chosen sentence almost perfectly. Dunefsky and Cohan \cite{r49} optimise a steering vector from a single example and study how far it generalises, including to harmful behaviour.
  \item \textbf{Adversarial examples.} Szegedy \textit{et al.} \cite{r50} and Goodfellow \textit{et al.} \cite{r51} showed that a small perturbation found by gradient descent can push a network to any output, and Zhang \textit{et al.} \cite{r52} showed that networks can fit random labels. This is the right comparison for our additive edit (Section~\ref{sec:additive-story}): once an optimiser has enough freedom, reaching the chosen output proves little.
\end{itemize}
As far as our limited search could tell, none of this work tunes one multiplier per \emph{SAE feature} for a single prompt with a side-effect term, or compares that with a hand-built feature sweep on held-out prompts.

\section{Comparing and Stitching Representations}

Model stitching joins the lower layers of one network to the upper layers of another through a small learned layer. If the stitched network still works, the two representations are compatible \cite{r41,r42}. Similarity measures such as CKA \cite{r43} compare activations without stitching. For language models, Chen \textit{et al.} \cite{r44} show that a simple affine map between the residual streams of two models moves features across at low cost. They carry SAEs from a smaller to a larger model, which cuts the larger SAE's training cost by about half, and they recover probes and steering vectors. Our translator (Chapter~\ref{ch:transfer}) is a ridge regression \cite{r45} of the same family, and we do not claim it as new.

\section{Research Gap}

Knowledge editing changes weights or the prompt \cite{r2,r3,r31}, SAE steering works inside one model \cite{r23,r28,r29}, and stitching work moves vectors learned in one model into another \cite{r44}. As far as a limited search could tell \cite{p11}, we did not find the following together: a joint mute-and-boost edit of causally ranked SAE features for a single fact, with a per-feature safety filter and pool refill; a comparison on held-out CounterFact prompts, with one yardstick, between a hand-built sweep and per-feature gradient descent; a control showing that an additive edit pushes unrelated words to rank 1; and a test of whether the change an edit makes in one model can be carried into another, with pass bars fixed beforehand. This report supplies these, negative results included. It does not claim that the SAE route beats prompting, probes, difference-of-means steering, ROME or in-context editing, because that comparison has not been made.
